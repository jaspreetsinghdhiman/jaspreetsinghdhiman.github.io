A RESEARCH-LED COLLECTION

Understanding the living heritage.

Punjabi is not only a language of everyday conversation. It is a living archive of place, work, devotion, humour, longing, protest and belonging. Its literary history is carried through oral performance, manuscripts, printed books, songs and contemporary writing.

How to read this collection

These are editorial introductions, not claims that every practice is identical across Punjab. Regional names, dates and customs vary. Individual records should be supported by reliable references, local knowledge and community review.

Punjabi terms are shown in Gurmukhi where appropriate. Shahmukhi and regional vocabulary can be added through reviewed contributions.

01

Two scripts, one language continuum

Punjabi is written in more than one script. Gurmukhi is widely used in Indian Punjab, while Shahmukhi is widely used in Pakistani Punjab. Script, region, schooling, publishing history and personal practice shape how people read and write. The archive presents both without treating either as the sole form of Punjabi.

EDITORIAL GUIDE
02

Oral literature and performance

Boliyan, tappay, vaaran, dhadi vaar, lok-geet, riddles and proverbs transmit knowledge beyond formal books. Their words and melodies can vary between villages, performers, occasions and generations. Recordings should preserve the performer’s name, location, language variety, date and consent.

EDITORIAL GUIDE
03

Poetry, Sufi traditions and modern writing

Punjabi literary traditions include devotional, Sufi, romantic, reformist and modern voices. Bulleh Shah, Waris Shah, Shah Hussain, Amrita Pritam, Shiv Kumar Batalvi and many other writers belong to distinct periods and contexts; their work should be introduced with reliable editions and historical framing rather than flattened into one story.

EDITORIAL GUIDE
04

A living language

Punjabi continues through theatre, music, journalism, education, digital publishing, diaspora communities and everyday speech. Contemporary language use is diverse: vocabulary, pronunciation and idiom shift across districts and communities.

01

Gurmukhi — ਗੁਰਮੁਖੀ

A script used widely for Punjabi in Indian Punjab; its history and standardisation should be studied through dated manuscripts and scholarship.

02

Shahmukhi — شاہ مکھی

A Perso-Arabic-based script widely used for Punjabi in Pakistani Punjab, with regional conventions and publishing traditions.

Lok-geet — ਲੋਕ-ਗੀਤ
