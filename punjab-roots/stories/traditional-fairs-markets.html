THE BIGGER PICTURE

Understanding the context.

Melas and bazaars were more than places to buy and sell. They brought neighbouring villages together, carried local skills into public view and created spaces for exchange, celebration and everyday conversation.

What this theme includes

Explore seasonal gatherings, religious observances, livestock and agricultural exchange, craft stalls, food traditions, folk performance and family visits. Their forms differ by district, community, faith and time.

Look closer

A bazaar can reveal how a town grew, what materials travelled through it, which craftspeople served local households and how streets connected neighbourhoods. Documenting a market means recording its name, location, period, trades, languages, memories and changes — not treating every bazaar as identical.

How we document it

Punjab’s fairs and markets continue to evolve. This archive distinguishes documented history from personal recollection and invites local knowledge to add context.

One district, many kinds of gathering

District records from Sangrur describe fairs with different local histories and rhythms. For example, the district administration records Mela Kuti at Gharachon and a fair at Longowal associated with the memory of Bhai Mani Singh. The same record describes folk performance and sporting events such as wrestling and kabaddi. These are examples from one district, not a complete calendar for all of Punjab.

What a careful record should capture

For each mela or bazaar, record the local name and spelling, village or town, district, season or date, associated shrine or institution where relevant, principal activities, vendors and crafts, languages used, and the source of each detail. If a date comes from an older district gazetteer or web page, label it as a historical record rather than a guaranteed current schedule.

Editorial promise

Punjab Roots labels oral memories, historical records and interpretation clearly. If a detail is uncertain or contested, that uncertainty stays visible.
