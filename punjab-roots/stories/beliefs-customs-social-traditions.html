THE BIGGER PICTURE

Understanding the context.

Traditions are carried through ordinary moments: welcoming a child, preparing a home, sharing food, marking a season, remembering elders and gathering as a family or neighbourhood.

What this theme includes

Explore customs through their context: who practises them, when they take place, what meanings people attach to them and how they change across generations. A custom may be shared widely, locally specific, or meaningful to one family alone.

Look closer

Social traditions are not fixed rules. They vary across region, faith, language, class, family and generation. The archive avoids presenting one community’s practice as universal and records disagreement or change where relevant.

How we document it

Personal testimony is valuable, but it is identified as testimony. The archive separates remembered experience, published research and claims that still need verification.

Customs are lived, not frozen

A custom may change when families move, occupations change, new technologies arrive or younger generations reinterpret an inherited practice. A record should therefore include the community and locality, the person who described it, the date of the account and whether the practice is still observed. Avoid describing a practice as universal simply because it is familiar in one household or village.

Questions that help preserve context

Who performs the custom? Is it connected to a life event, season, occupation, faith or local history? Are there different names or forms in nearby districts? Who does not practise it, and why? These questions make room for diversity, change and disagreement without treating any one account as the only version.

Editorial promise

Punjab Roots labels oral memories, historical records and interpretation clearly. If a detail is uncertain or contested, that uncertainty stays visible.
