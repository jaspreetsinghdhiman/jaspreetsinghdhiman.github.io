THE BIGGER PICTURE

Understanding the context.

The 1947 Partition divided Punjab and transformed everyday life across the region. Families were displaced, communities experienced violence and loss, and millions had to rebuild homes and relationships in new places.

What this theme includes

Partition memories can include journeys, abandoned homes, changed borders, missing relatives, new beginnings, language, food, objects kept by families and stories passed between generations. No single account represents everyone’s experience.

Look closer

Oral histories are shared with care. Memories are attributed to the person or family who provided them, sensitive details are not published without permission, and differing recollections are not forced into one version.

How we document it

The archive distinguishes documented historical context from personal testimony. It avoids graphic sensationalism, communal blame and unsupported casualty or migration figures.

A short chronology of 1947

On 3 June 1947, the British government announced the plan to transfer power to two successor states. India and Pakistan marked independence on 14 and 15 August. The Punjab boundary award was made public on 17 August, after independence celebrations had already begun. The new line divided districts, transport links, farms, towns, sacred places and family networks. The months around Partition brought mass displacement and severe violence; people crossed the new border in both directions.

Read documents beside memories

Official files, boundary maps, newspapers and administrative reports can help establish dates and decisions, but they do not capture every household’s experience. Oral histories preserve details that formal records often omit: a station platform, a key kept after leaving a home, a neighbour who helped, a changed village name or a journey remembered decades later. Each testimony should be attributed with consent and read as one person’s account, not as a universal explanation.

Care when collecting testimony

Ask permission before recording, storing or publishing a story. Let narrators decide which names and details may be shared. Do not pressure people to recount traumatic events, and do not publish identifying details about living relatives without consent. Preserve the narrator’s words faithfully; translations should be labelled and checked by a fluent speaker.

Editorial promise

Punjab Roots labels oral memories, historical records and interpretation clearly. If a detail is uncertain or contested, that uncertainty stays visible.
